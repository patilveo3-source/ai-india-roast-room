# Round 1: Opening positions

## Buffett

SEAT: Buffett (Money and capital)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on B)
OPTION SCORES: A 4/10 · B 8/10 · C 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way (until the wedding day, then close to one-way)

THE CALL: B. Get engaged now, then marry in 12 to 18 months, once the money talk, a shared lease and budget, and the goals check are done and written down.

WHY:
1. Never risk ruin (S095). My brain has no recorded view on picking a spouse, so this is my own translation: marriage is the one merger you can't unwind cheaply. I'll assume $4,000 a month in expenses. That makes your savings $12,000 to $24,000. Add a $25,000 wedding (also my assumption) six months out, and that cushion is gone before any checking has been done. A divorce later could halve what's left, plus legal fees. Option A spends the margin of safety (S065) first and checks second.
2. Important and knowable (S022). The things that decide a marriage's money and its ten-year odds are money habits, children, location and careers. You can find all four out in months, and you haven't discussed any of them. Four honest talks and a year on one budget cost very little to check a contract meant to last 50 years.
3. People filter, integrity first (S025, S035). Watch how your partner handles a bad month, a fight, an overdraft. Judge by what they do over time, not how the first happy year feels. "Acquisitions are similar to marriage: They start, of course, with a joyful wedding" (S092). The wedding is the easy part.
Option C fails as well. You already think highly of this person, and making them wait two or three years risks a mistake of omission (S028): the fat pitch walks off the plate while you stand there.

THE RISK I SEE: The checks turn into theater. At month nine a red flag shows up on money or kids, and you marry anyway because the venue is booked. "The most important thing to do when you find yourself in a hole is to stop digging." (S063)

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Proof that you two have already shared a budget for 6 months or more, shown each other every debt and credit score, and agreed on kids and city. Then I'd move A to 7/10.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Sit down together and swap full personal balance sheets: income, debts, credit scores, savings and monthly spending.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: When you and your partner last disagreed about money, how did they handle it, and would you sign up for 50 more years of exactly that?

## Bezos

SEAT: Bezos (Strategy and decisions)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 5/10 · B 8/10 · C 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Option B. Get engaged now, then marry in 12 to 18 months once the money, children, location and career talks are done and you have lived together.

WHY:
1. Sort the door first. Marriage is a one-way door. Engagement is close to a two-way door. One-way doors "must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation." (Type 1 and Type 2 decisions, S063). Option B makes the reversible commitment now and saves the irreversible one for after the checks.
2. The 70 percent rule says you decide at about 70 percent of what you wish you knew (S064). The brief says you haven't yet had the money, kids, location and career talks. My guess is that puts you at about 50 percent today. Twelve to eighteen months of real checks gets you past 70. Waiting two to three years, as C does, is waiting for 90: "If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow." (S064)
3. Regret minimization. At 80, would you regret not committing to this person? You say you feel ready, and "Most regrets, by the way, are acts of omission and not commission." (S043). That is why I score C low. Engaging now commits to the person, so you aren't left with that regret, while the date stays open.
4. Never bet the company. I'm assuming savings of about 3 to 6 months of expenses and a wedding that costs $15,000 to $30,000. A rushed six-month wedding could use up all of that cushion. Draining the cushion turns a reversible spend into a one-way strain right as the marriage starts (S018). Option A can work, and plenty of couples do it well. I still pick the path that removes the biggest risks one at a time, before you go through the one-way door (systematic risk elimination, S008).

THE RISK I SEE: The checks become a formality. You get engaged, never have the hard money and children talk, and walk through the one-way door at 50 percent.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If you two have already lived together and fully agree on money, children and location, you are past 70 percent. Then A is fine.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Each of you writes one page on money, children, where you'll live and careers over the next ten years. Swap the pages and read them together without talking first.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Ten years from now, what will you both still want that won't change, and have you said it out loud to each other?

## Leila

SEAT: Leila (Leadership and scaling teams)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on B)
OPTION SCORES: A 4/10 · B 8/10 · C 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: B. Get engaged now and set the wedding 12 to 18 months out, with the checks written down and dated, not just felt.

WHY:
1. Define good before you judge. "if you don't properly Define and verbalize your expectations then nobody knows how to win or lose the game they don't even know how to play the game" (S034). The brief assumes there has been no detailed talk about money, children, location, or careers. Feeling ready is not the same as having stated expectations. Right now they would be signing up for a two-person partnership with no roles agreed.
2. Partner rule. "if you don't agree, don't move forward." (S008). In every partnership I have seen work, the roles were set first: "we clearly define our roles and then we give each other autonomy to make decisions in those areas." (S008). The engagement period is when you get those agreements done.
3. Reversibility tax (Power of a decision, S016). "things that are easy to reverse rarely ever need much thought put into them." (S016). Marriage is one of the hardest things to reverse. Being engaged is a commitment you can still unwind. So go for "the most probable compounding positive outcome plus lowest risk" (S044). C is too slow: with a relationship 1 to 3 years in, waiting adds time but no information. A skips the test.

THE RISK I SEE: They treat the engagement as wedding planning and never have the hard conversations, then marry in month 14 with the same untested assumptions.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If they have already lived together for 6 or more months and agreed in writing on money, children, and location, I would move to A at 7/10.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Write a one-page list with them: what a good marriage looks like in year 10, plus four things you both agree on (money, kids, city, careers). Put a check-in on the calendar every month.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: When you two last disagreed about something that mattered, how did it get settled, and did you both stick with the decision?

The rule: agree on what good looks like before you sign anything you can't undo.

## Hormozi

SEAT: Hormozi (Offers, pricing, and sales)
STANCE: Yes (on B)
OPTION SCORES: A 5/10 · B 8/10 · C 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: B, with a variant: get engaged this month, set a date about 12 months out, and pay for the wedding in cash from money saved after the engagement.

WHY:
1. The constraint here isn't "am I ready." It's that the money talk hasn't happened yet. On a sales call, obstacles come up before price: circumstances, other people, myself (Obstacles before objections, S009, S074). The wedding is the price. The talk about money, kids, location, and careers is the obstacles. Option A shows the price before you've done discovery.
2. The math, with my assumptions: $4K a month in expenses, $18K saved, $1K a month of new savings, and a $30K wedding. Option A gives you about $24K by the wedding date against a $30K bill. That's $6K of debt, zero emergency fund, and you start the marriage strained. Option B at 15 months gives you $33K. Spend $15K in cash and keep $18K as a reserve. I'm "very close to the Ramsey side" (S106), and "everything I've bought to this point in my life has been cash." (S056). Pay cash and start clean.
3. Partners: I had nine failed partnerships before the one that worked, and the lesson was complementary skills, aligned values, and write it all down (Partners, S083). Waiting 2 to 3 years (C) doesn't fix anything. It's dithering wearing a career costume. "perfect timing is a complete myth. But perfect preparation isn't." (S058). You don't need more time. You need the checks.

THE RISK I SEE: The money talk turns up a real mismatch, like one spender and one saver or hidden debt, and they skip past it because the engagement is already announced.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Two things move me to A with a cheap wedding: a written budget both people agree to, and a wedding under $10K paid in cash with 6 months of expenses still in the bank afterward. If they can't agree on a budget in two conversations, I move toward C.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Do one back-of-napkin session together. Both put income, debt, savings, and monthly spending on one page, then write down the wedding budget and the date that budget allows.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: If your partner's numbers showed $20K of debt you didn't know about, would you still sign this week, and what would the plan be?
